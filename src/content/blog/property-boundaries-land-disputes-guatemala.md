In Guatemala the boundary drawn on the ground and the boundary written in your title can disagree, and that gap is where most land disputes start. Before you buy near Antigua Guatemala, three things have to line up: what the escritura says, what the Property Registry holds, and what a surveyor measures on the ground. When they agree, ownership is clean. When they do not, you inherit a problem the seller never mentioned.

Here is how boundaries are proven, how disputes are resolved, and what to verify first.

## Why do boundary disputes happen in Guatemala?

Most boundary trouble is inherited, not invented. A colonial-era finca gets divided among heirs, the family agrees who takes which strip over coffee and never registers it, and twenty years later two people hold paperwork describing overlapping ground.

Other common causes:

- **Approximate measurements in old deeds.** Many escrituras describe land as "más o menos" a size, with the line running "from the old ceiba tree to the García wall." Trees die, walls move, and the description stops working.
- **Unregistered subdivisions.** Land is sold by private contract with a hand-drawn plan never filed at the Registry, so the official record still shows one big parcel.
- **Gradual encroachment.** A neighbour builds a wall a few inches over the line, plants a hedge further along, and after ten quiet years the shift becomes the new normal.
- **Municipal changes.** Road widenings and public works can shift access or clip frontage without the Registry being updated.
- **Unresolved inheritance.** Property still registered to a grandparent who died intestate, with several relatives claiming shares.

## Which documents actually define your boundary?

Four records matter, and they do not always agree:

1. **The escritura pública.** The public deed signed at the notary, stating location, area and boundaries.
2. **The plano.** The survey plan, drawn by a registered surveyor, showing measurements, orientation and neighbouring parcels. A plano with a Registry stamp beats a hand sketch.
3. **The Registro General de la Propiedad entry.** The official record that legally proves ownership. It should describe the same parcel as the deed and plan.
4. **Municipal records.** The catastro (used for IUSI tax) and municipal plans, which show frontage, access and zoning.

The registry entry governs ownership, but it can carry old or approximate measurements. That is why a fresh survey matters: it turns a legal description into real, defensible numbers.

## How do you verify boundaries before you buy?

Work through these steps in order, and do not skip the ones that cost money.

1. **Order a certified title search (certificación registral).** Your lawyer pulls the current entry and its history: owner, liens, and any registered servidumbres.
2. **Hire your own surveyor.** Use an independent ingeniero topógrafo or agrimensor, not one recommended by the seller. A surveyor who reports to you tells the truth.
3. **Walk the perimeter with the neighbours.** The colindantes are the people who will dispute your line later. When they stand at the corners with you and accept the markers, a future conflict becomes far less likely.
4. **Compare the survey against the deed and registry.** Any difference in area, frontage or orientation must be explained, corrected by notarial act, or treated as a red flag.
5. **Check the servidumbres.** Access routes, drainage rights and utility easements must be registered, not just agreed verbally.
6. **Confirm frontage and access with the municipality.** Make sure it recognises the access the seller is promising you.

## What are the most common types of land disputes?

| Dispute | What it looks like | How it is resolved |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Encroachment | A neighbour's wall, building or fence sits over the line | Notarised boundary agreement, or removal |
| Overlapping titles | Two deeds describe part of the same ground | Registry review, survey, then civil action |
| Unregistered right of way | Access across a neighbour's land never filed | Notarised servidumbre, or buying the strip |
| Boundary shift after inheritance | Heirs disagree where the lines fall | Family partition recorded by notary |
| Adverse possession (posesión) | Someone occupies and works the land for years | Court action; hard to reverse |
| Municipal expropriation | A road project clips your frontage | Compensation claim through the municipality |

The disputes that reach court are the ones nobody wrote down.

## What is a servidumbre de paso, and why does it matter?

A servidumbre de paso is a legal right of way across someone else's property. Around Antigua many lots reach the road only across a neighbour's field, and that access is often agreed verbally and never registered.

Buy a lot whose only access is an unregistered servidumbre and you may own land you cannot legally reach. Confirm the right of way is written into both deeds, registered at the Registry, and wide enough for vehicles and construction. If it is not registered, make registering it a condition of the purchase.

## How are boundary disputes resolved in Guatemala?

- **Notarial agreement.** The parties fix the line in a notarial act (deslinde) and update the Registry. Fast and inexpensive.
- **Mediation.** A neutral third party helps neighbours reach terms, useful when the relationship still exists.
- **Civil action.** A juicio ordinario decides an unclear line but can take years and burn legal fees. That is why you verify before buying, not after.

## Red flags that should stop a purchase

- No plano with a Registry stamp.
- The deed area does not match the registry entry.
- The neighbours refuse to meet you at the corners.
- Access depends on a verbal agreement with a third party.
- The property is still in a deceased owner's name with unresolved heirs.
- The seller pushes you to skip the survey "to save time."
