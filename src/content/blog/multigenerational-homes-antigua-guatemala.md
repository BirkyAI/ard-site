Antigua Guatemala is one of the easier places in Central America to settle a family across three generations: grandparents, parents and children, either under one roof or a short walk apart. The reasons are practical rather than sentimental. Most colonial homes are single-storey and wrapped around a courtyard, the historic centre is flat and walkable, domestic help and home healthcare are close and affordable, and the climate never forces a fight with heating or air conditioning. The honest caveats are the same ones that affect any older buyer: cobblestone streets, entrance steps, narrow bathroom doors, and walls 40 to 80 cm thick that are hard to modify. Here is how families actually do it, and what it costs in 2026.

## Why Antigua Guatemala Suits Multigenerational Living

Three things stand out. First, the layout: a colonial house is a ring of rooms around a patio, so each generation can take a wing and still share one courtyard, and a casita or old service room can become a private unit. Second, support: domestic help runs well below North American rates, and clinics, pharmacies and a private hospital sit minutes from the centre. Third, daily life: a flat 12-block core means an 80-year-old can walk to the market and a teenager can walk to Spanish class without a car.

The limit is mobility inside old houses. Many have an entrance step, interior thresholds and 60 to 70 cm bathroom doors. If stairs, wide doors and level floors matter to your family, read our guide to [accessible living in Antigua Guatemala](/blog/accessible-living-antigua-guatemala/) before you shortlist anything.

## What Layout Works for Three Generations?

| Option | Best for | Typical 2026 price |
|:--|:--|:--|
| One large colonial with a casita or service wing | Family that wants a shared courtyard but separate living quarters | $250,000 to $600,000 |
| Main house plus a new casita built at the rear | Adding a self-contained unit for grandparents | $23,000 to $58,000 to build |
| Two homes on one lot or adjacent lots | Two households that want full independence | $150,000 to $400,000 combined |
| House in a gated community with a guest suite | Security, amenities and a turnkey move-in | $180,000 to $450,000 |
| A finca with more than one house | Extended family that also wants land | $300,000 to $1,000,000+ |

The single most common setup is a restored colonial with a small casita off the courtyard. One couple takes the main house, the grandparents take the casita, and the patio becomes the shared room that makes the arrangement feel like one home rather than two.

## Which Rooms Decide Whether It Works?

- **A ground-floor bedroom with a full bathroom.** This is the feature to buy, not to add. If it does not exist, budget to convert a room.
- **A separate entrance**, even a side door onto the courtyard, so two households keep two schedules.
- **A second kitchen or at least a kitchenette**, so cooking is never a negotiation.
- **Storage.** Colonial homes were built with almost none, and three generations bring three households of belongings.
- **A quiet room away from the street.** The room nearest the road will always be the least wanted.
- **A courtyard that pulls everyone together.** The point of the layout is a shared dinner, so check it can host one.

Anything that changes what the street sees, including a new front door or a raised wall, needs a municipal licence and a CNPAG heritage opinion. Interior work behind the front door normally does not. ## Should You Buy One House or Two?

A single house with a casita keeps one title, one set of taxes and one courtyard, and it suits a family that wants togetherness. Two separate homes give more privacy and are easier to sell apart, but they double your carrying costs: two IUSI bills, two insurance policies, two maintenance budgets. A middle route most families take is a main house plus a casita on the same deed, which can later be rented out as income or reconfigured as the family changes.

Decide before you buy. Adding a second kitchen and a courtyard entrance later is straightforward. Splitting one title into two is slow, expensive and sometimes not possible.

## How Do You Handle Ownership and Title as a Family?

In Guatemala every buyer named on the escritura holds a defined share, and foreign owners need no residency to buy. If two generations share the purchase, set the percentages at the deed and put a written co-ownership agreement in place, because Guatemala has no right of survivorship, so a deceased co-owner's share passes to their heirs rather than automatically to the survivor. For parents who want the house to stay in the family, a Guatemalan testamento avoids an intestate scramble. If one generation is buying from abroad, a power of attorney lets a trusted representative sign without flying in.

## What Support Makes Daily Life Easier?

- **Domestic help:** a monthly salary well below US rates covers cleaning, cooking and elder companionship.
- **Healthcare:** private clinics and a hospital are minutes from the centre, and in-home nursing is affordable.
- **Schools and language:** international schools and one-on-one Spanish programmes both operate in and around Antigua.
- **Getting around:** the flat centre is walkable, and tuk-tuks cost Q5 to Q15 for short hops.

## A Multigenerational Buying Checklist

1. Confirm a ground-floor bedroom and full bathroom, or budget to convert one.
2. Count the doors: 80 cm clears a walker, 60 cm does not.
3. Decide whether you need a separate entrance and a second kitchen before you buy.
4. Check the courtyard shape: can two households share it without living in each other's way?
5. Agree the ownership percentages and put them in the escritura.
6. Walk the route your parents will walk, then walk it again at 6 pm.
