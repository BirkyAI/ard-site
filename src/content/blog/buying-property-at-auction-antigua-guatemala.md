# Buying Property at Auction in Antigua Guatemala

Yes, you can buy property at auction in Guatemala, including as a foreigner, but it looks nothing like the bargain hunting you see on television. A Guatemalan auction is a remate judicial, a court-ordered sale used to pay a debt, and the process, the risks and the timeline are all set by a judge rather than a real estate agent. Handled carefully it can put a property in your hands below market value. Handled casually it can leave you owning a building you cannot enter.

Here is how remates actually work, who is allowed to bid, and where the real risks sit.

## What is a remate judicial in Guatemala?

A remate is a public auction ordered by a court when a debtor cannot pay and a creditor asks the judge to seize and sell a property to recover the money. The court fixes an appraisal, the avalúo, sets the auction date, and publishes a legal notice, an edicto, in the Diario de Centro América and sometimes in a local newspaper. Anyone legally able to buy real estate in Guatemala can submit a written bid, called a postura. This is a court procedure, not a broker listing, so the usual marketing, viewings and back-and-forth negotiation simply do not exist.

## How does a Guatemalan property auction actually work?

A remate runs in stages, and the minimum price is set by law rather than by a seller. In the first remate, bids must reach at least two thirds of the court appraisal. If nobody bids, a second remate follows and the floor drops to half of the appraisal, which is where genuine bargains appear.

Your written postura must come with a cheque de caja, a cashier's check, as a deposit, commonly around ten percent of your offer, held by the court until the auction resolves. If you win, the judge issues an adjudication, the property is transferred by escritura pública before a notary, and the title is registered at the Registro General de la Propiedad. From filing to actual possession, expect several months, and longer if the losing side appeals. Confirm the current minimum-bid percentages and deposit rules with a Guatemalan attorney before you commit, because these figures are set by law and do change.

## Can foreigners bid at a Guatemalan auction?

Yes. A foreigner can own property in Guatemala, and bidding at a remate is open to anyone able to complete the purchase. Two practical constraints matter more than your nationality. First, you generally need to pay in cash, because banks do not lend against an auction property you do not yet own. Second, you need a local bank account or a local cheque de caja, a notary and an attorney, and a Guatemalan tax number, the NIT, for the filing.

Location matters too. Guatemala restricts certain ownership in the strip near international borders and along the coast, so a property in the wrong place can carry extra conditions. Around Antigua this is rarely an issue, but it is one more reason to have your lawyer verify the specific property before you place a bid.

## What are the real risks of buying at auction?

The discount exists because the risks are real, and most of them are invisible in the auction notice.

- **The property may be occupied.** A tenant or the former owner can still be living there, and removing them, a desalojo, takes time and legal fees you pay yourself.
- **You buy it as it is.** There is no inspection and no seller disclosure. You are bidding on a description and an appraisal, not on a building you have examined.
- **Debts can follow the property.** Unpaid property tax, the IUSI, and utility arrears may need to be settled before the title comes clean.
- **Title problems surface late.** A lien, an easement dispute or a family claim can delay or complicate the registry entry.
- **Timelines slip.** Appeals and procedural objections can push possession out by many months while your money stays tied up.

## Bank repossessions and off-market deals

Judicial auctions are the most dramatic route to a distressed price, but they are not the only one, and often not the safest.

Guatemalan banks regularly sell properties they have repossessed, known as bienes adjudicados, from their own published lists. The title is usually already clean, the bank is a motivated but professional seller, and the closing looks like a normal purchase. The trade-off is a smaller discount.

| Route | How you find it | Typical price | Main risk | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Judicial remate | Court edictos in the Diario de Centro América | 50 to 90 percent of appraisal | Occupants and delays | Patient cash buyers |
| Bank repossession | Bank lists of bienes adjudicados | 70 to 90 percent of market | Sold as is, limited viewings | Buyers who want a clean title |
| Off-market distressed | Attorneys and local agents | Negotiated, 10 to 25 percent below market | Slow, uncertain seller | Buyers who want a normal closing |

## Where do you find distressed property in Guatemala?

The honest answer is that the best deals never reach a public notice. Judicial auctions are announced in the Diario de Centro América and on courthouse boards, and bank repossessions are posted on the banks' own pages. But the properties most worth buying are found through attorneys, notaries and agents who hear that a seller is under pressure before anything is published. In Antigua, where good colonial houses rarely reach an open listing, that network is the whole game.
