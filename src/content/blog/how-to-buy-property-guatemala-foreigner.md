For decades, foreigners have been drawn to Guatemala's colonial cities, volcanic landscapes, and affordable cost of living. Antigua Guatemala, in particular, has become a hotspot for expats, retirees, and remote workers looking to buy property.

If you're a foreigner wondering **how to buy property in Guatemala**, the short answer is: **yes, you can — and the process is surprisingly straightforward.** Guatemala has no restrictions on foreign ownership for most properties, and the legal system protects buyers who follow the right steps.

This guide walks you through exactly what you need to know, from legal requirements to closing costs, so you can buy with confidence.

## Can Foreigners Buy Property in Guatemala?

**Yes.** Guatemala places no restrictions on foreign nationals buying real estate. You do not need to be a resident, a citizen, or even live in the country to purchase property.

There is one important exception: **land within 15 km of international borders or 50 km of the coastline** is subject to special restrictions. For most buyers looking at Antigua (which is inland and well away from borders), this does not apply.

Properties in Antigua Guatemala, Guatemala City, Lake Atitlán, and most highland areas are fully accessible to foreign buyers with the same rights as Guatemalan citizens.

## Step 1: Find a Trusted Real Estate Agent in Antigua

The single most important step is finding a reputable agent who specializes in working with foreign buyers. A good agent will:

- Verify property titles and legal status before you make an offer
- Connect you with a local notary (*notario*) who handles the legal transfer
- Negotiate in Spanish on your behalf
- Help you navigate cultural differences in the buying process

Look for agents who are members of the **Cámara Guatemalteca de Bienes Raíces (CGBR)** — similar to a local real estate board. Ask for client references, especially from other foreign buyers.

## Step 2: Make an Offer and Sign a Preliminary Agreement

Once you find a property, your agent presents an offer to the seller. If accepted, both parties sign a **promesa de compraventa** (preliminary purchase agreement).

This document outlines:
- Purchase price and payment schedule
- Deposit amount (typically 10–20%)
- Closing date
- Contingencies (e.g., clear title verification)

At this stage, you pay the deposit. The property is taken off the market while the legal due diligence begins.

## Step 3: Title Search and Due Diligence

Your *notario* (a licensed attorney, not a US-style notary) performs a **title search** at the General Property Registry (*Registro General de la Propiedad*). This confirms:

- The seller is the legal owner
- There are no liens, encumbrances, or pending legal issues
- Property taxes are up to date
- The property boundaries match what's being sold

This is non-negotiable. **Never skip the title search**, even if the seller seems trustworthy. The *notario* also verifies zoning, usage restrictions, and any municipal permits.

This process typically takes 2–4 weeks.

## Step 4: Sign the Final Deed (*Escritura Pública*)

Once the title is clean and all conditions are met, both parties sign the **escritura pública** (public deed) before a Guatemalan notary. This is the official transfer of ownership.

Your *notario* drafts the deed and registers it with the Property Registry. After registration, you receive a **certified copy** of the deed, which is your proof of ownership.

The deed is legally binding and valid internationally.

## Step 5: Register at the Property Registry

The final legal step is **registration**. Your *notario* submits the deed to the *Registro General de la Propiedad*. Once registered (typically 2–4 weeks after signing), the property is legally yours.

At this point, you'll also need to:
- Register for the **municipal property tax (IUSI)** — the *notario* usually handles this
- Set up utility accounts (water, electricity, internet)
- If you plan to rent the property, register your rental agreement with the municipality

## Costs of Buying Property in Guatemala

| Cost Item | Typical Amount | Who Pays |
|---|---|---|
| **Property price** | Varies | Buyer |
| **Notary fees** | 1–2% of purchase price | Buyer |
| **Property registration** | ~0.5–1% of purchase price | Buyer |
| **Title search and due diligence** | $500–$1,500 | Buyer |
| **Real estate commission** | 3–5% | Seller (usually) |
| **Property transfer tax (plusvalía)** | 3% of registered value | Seller (usually) |
| **Municipal tax (IUSI)** | ~0.2–0.6% annually | Buyer |

**Total closing costs for the buyer** typically range from **2–4% of the purchase price**.

As a rule of thumb, on a $200,000 property in Antigua, expect to pay roughly **$4,000–$8,000** in buyer-side closing costs.

## Financing Options for Foreign Buyers

Getting a mortgage as a foreigner in Guatemala is possible but not always straightforward. Most foreign buyers pay in cash or finance through their home country.

**Options include:**

- **Cash purchase** — The most common route. Clean, fast, and gives you negotiating leverage.
- **Seller financing** — Some sellers offer financing directly, typically with a 30–50% down payment and 5–10 year terms.
- **International mortgages** — A few Guatemalan banks offer mortgages to foreigners, but interest rates are higher (9–14%) and require 40–50% down.
- **Home-country financing** — Some buyers take out a home equity loan or line of credit from their home country and pay cash in Guatemala.

For most buyers, **cash or seller financing** is the most practical path.

## Should You Use a Corporation or Buy as an Individual?

Many foreign buyers in Antigua purchase property as individuals. It's simpler and cheaper.

However, some buyers form a **Guatemalan corporation (sociedad anónima)** for:
- Liability protection
- Easier inheritance transfer
- Multiple investors sharing ownership
- Rental property management at scale

Your *notario* can advise on which structure makes sense for your situation. Corporation formation takes 2–4 weeks and costs approximately $500–$1,000 in legal fees.

## Common Mistakes Foreign Buyers Make

1. **Skipping the title search** — The most expensive mistake. Always verify ownership before transferring money.
2. **Not verifying zoning** — That colonial mansion might be zoned residential-only. If you want to run a B&B, you need commercial zoning.
3. **Assuming "affordable" means good value** — A cheap property with title problems or structural issues is far more expensive in the long run.
4. **Not budgeting for closing costs** — 2–4% on top of the purchase price catches many buyers off guard.
5. **Using an agent who doesn't specialize in foreign buyers** — General agents may not understand the specific needs of international purchasers.
