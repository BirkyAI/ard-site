# Courtyard Gardens in Antigua Guatemala Homes

In Antigua Guatemala the garden is not the backyard. Colonial houses are built around an interior patio, so the courtyard is the first thing you see when the door opens and the room you actually use most evenings. Bougainvillea spilling over a white wall, a fountain you can hear from the kitchen, a lime tree beside the outdoor table: that is the part of a home buyers remember.

This guide covers what grows here, what landscaping costs in 2026, how the seasons change the work, and how to keep a courtyard alive from abroad.

## Why the Courtyard Matters More Than the Lot

Antigua sits at roughly 1,530 meters, high enough that frost never arrives and mild enough that something is in bloom all year. Colonial buildings turn inward for a reason: thick adobe and stone walls block street noise, and the patio is the only place a house gets direct sun and open sky.

The courtyard also cools the home. Shade from a tree and the movement of water drop the temperature by several degrees, and the cross-ventilation that colonial homes depend on works only when the patio side stays planted rather than paved over. A courtyard with mature planting feels five degrees cooler on an April afternoon than the same house with bare stone.

For buyers, a finished garden is one of the few improvements that holds its value at resale. It photographs better, it rents better, and it is the feature guests mention first.

## What Grows Well in an Antigua Guatemala Garden?

Antigua has two seasons: dry from November to April and rainy from May to October. Plants that struggle here need constant moisture or cannot take a heavy afternoon downpour. These do well in almost any courtyard:

| Plant | Light | Water | Why it works here |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Bougainvillea | Full sun | Low | Flowers hardest in the dry season, covers a wall in two years |
| Ciprés (cypress) | Full sun | Medium | Vertical green, traditionally used to frame an entrance |
| Citrus (lime, orange) | Full sun | Medium | Fruit, scent and shade in one tree, pots well on a patio |
| Ferns and helechos | Shade | High | Fill the damp shaded corner that kills everything else |
| Jade plant | Full to part sun | Very low | Nearly indestructible, holds up through dry months |
| Jacaranda | Full sun | Medium | Purple bloom in spring, needs space and a deep root run |

Herbs do well year round in pots: basil, mint, parsley and rosemary all thrive. Roses grow but need attention in the rainy season, when black spot appears fast.

## Designing a Colonial Courtyard

The courtyard is a small room outdoors, so design it the same way you would design a room. A few rules that hold up in Antigua:

- **Anchor the space with one feature.** A fountain, a mature tree or a single large pot. More than one centerpiece makes a patio feel crowded.
- **Keep the paving simple.** Stone, brick or compacted pebble, mixed with planted beds. Full concrete looks hot and bounces noise back at the house.
- **Layer the height.** Ground cover, mid-size shrubs, then one tall element such as cypress or a pergola with vines.
- **Think about the azotea.** Many Antigua homes open onto a roof terrace. If the garden is on the ground and the terrace is above, plant the taller trees where they will be seen from upstairs.
- **Light it at night.** Warm low fixtures at the base of a tree or behind a fountain do more for a courtyard than any daytime feature.

## Two Seasons, Two Different Problems

### Rainy season (May to October)

Daily afternoon rain keeps everything lush and brings the season's problems: standing water, mosquitoes, mold on north-facing walls and gutters that clog with leaves. Check drainage before the rains start, clear gutters, and prune anything that touches the house so air can move. Water features need cleaning more often because algae grows quickly.

### Dry season (November to April)

Now the work flips. Plants need consistent irrigation, dust collects on leaves, and newly planted trees need water until their roots are established, usually one to two years. This is also the season bougainvillea and most flowering shrubs look their best, so irrigation pays off visually. Drip lines on a timer handle a courtyard well and use far less water than a hose.

Prices below are typical 2026 ranges in quetzales, based on local nurseries and garden crews. Small courtyards cost far less than a full property, so treat these as starting points.

## Hiring a Gardener

Most Antigua homes work with the same gardener for years, often one day a week or every two weeks. Ask neighbors and your property manager for a name, agree a fixed monthly rate, and be specific about what the visit covers. If you have a fountain, palms or a mature tree, confirm the gardener has handled those before.

## Keeping a Garden Alive While You Are Abroad

An unwatered courtyard in April goes brown in three weeks, and gutters left uncleared through the rains can damage a roof. If you own from abroad, put garden care inside a property management agreement rather than hoping a neighbor will help: a fixed schedule with photos after each visit, someone to check irrigation, and a plan for storm season are what keep a courtyard looking owner-occupied.

## A Garden Before You Sell

If a sale is coming, spend on the courtyard before the photographer arrives. Cut back overgrowth, pressure-wash the paving, add fresh pots and fix broken lighting. It is one of the cheapest ways to lift listing photos and signals a well-kept home.

## Start With the Patio You Have

You do not need a designer to make a courtyard come alive. Fix drainage, plant one bougainvillea against a sunny wall, and a plain patio becomes the best room in the house.
