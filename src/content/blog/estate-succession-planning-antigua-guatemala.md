Owning a home in Antigua Guatemala is one of the most rewarding investments you can make. But there is a question most buyers postpone until it becomes urgent: **what happens to your property when you pass away or can no longer manage it?**

Guatemalan inheritance law works very differently from the common-law systems of the United States, the United Kingdom or Canada. If you hold title in your own name with no plan in place, your family may face years of paperwork, mandatory-heir rules that divide the property in ways you never intended, and unexpected costs. The good news is that a little planning now saves your heirs a great deal of stress later.

This guide explains how Guatemala handles inherited property, what a *testamento* covers, and the tools — from trusts to corporations — that let you pass your Antigua home to exactly who you want.

## How Does Guatemalan Inheritance Law Work?

Guatemala follows **forced-heirship (herederos forzosos)** rules. Unlike most U.S. states, where you can freely leave assets to anyone, Guatemalan law reserves a fixed portion of your estate for your **legal heirs** — typically your spouse and your children.

This means:

- If you have a Guatemalan *testamento*, a share of the estate is still set aside for forced heirs.
- If you die *without* a will (intestate), the courts divide your property according to a strict legal ranking of heirs.
- Disinheriting a spouse or child outright is much harder in Guatemala than in many common-law countries.

For foreign owners, the first question is usually: "Does my U.S., U.K. or Canadian will cover my Guatemalan property?" The short answer is **partly**. Most lawyers recommend a separate Guatemalan will executed before a *notario* so your local assets follow one clear, locally recognized document.

## What Is a Testamento in Guatemala?

A *testamento* is a Guatemalan will, drawn up before a **notary public (*notario*)** and registered so it is valid in the local legal system. It is the single most important document for any foreign owner.

A well-drafted *testamento* can:

- Name specific heirs for your Antigua property.
- Simplify the transfer when you pass, avoiding a contested intestate succession.
- Coordinate with your home-country will so the two documents do not conflict.

Creating one usually takes a few sessions with a *notario* and costs a fraction of the legal fees your heirs would pay to sort out an intestate estate. If you already closed on a property, you likely worked with a *notario* — the same professional can advise you on your will.

## What Are the Inheritance Costs and Taxes?

Planning ahead also helps your heirs budget. Common costs when a property changes hands by inheritance include:

- **Notarial fees** for the legal transfer of title.
- **Registration** at the *Registro de la Propiedad* (property registry).
- **Inheritance/succession and capital-gains considerations**, depending on how the property is valued at the date of transfer.
- **IUSI**, the annual municipal property tax, which continues to apply each year.

None of these are deal-breakers for a single family home, but they are far easier to plan for than to discover after the fact. A local advisor can give you a realistic estimate based on your property's registered value.

## Your Options for Passing Property Smoothly

There is no single right answer — it depends on your family situation, your home country and how much privacy you want. The main structures are:

| Structure | When it helps most | Key trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Testamento (Guatemalan will)** | Simple inheritance by spouse/children | Forced-heir rules still apply to a share |
| **Fideicomiso (Guatemalan trust)** | Privacy, succession planning, shielding assets | Higher setup and administration cost |
| **Sociedad anónima (corporation)** | Holding property for an income-producing asset | Annual maintenance and formalities |
| **Joint ownership with right of survivorship** | Avoiding probate between two partners | Laws vary by how title is registered |

Every row above answers a slightly different question. The *testamento* is the general starting point; trusts and corporations are worth exploring for higher-value or income-producing properties.

## What Should You Do Now?

You do not need a dramatic restructure to get started. Focus on these five steps:

1. **Make a Guatemalan will.** Book one session with a trusted *notario* to draft your *testamento*.
2. **Keep your documents together.** Store your *escritura* (notarized deed), registry folio and IDs where family can find them.
3. **Name your beneficiaries clearly.** Decide who should receive the property and tell them.
4. **Tell your local agent.** A Antigua Real Estate Development advisor can flag anything unusual about your title during their listings.
5. **Review every 3–5 years.** Update your *testamento* after major life changes like marriage, divorce or a new child.
