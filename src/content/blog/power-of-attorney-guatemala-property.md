Yes, you can buy or sell property in Guatemala without flying down, by granting a power of attorney (poder) to someone you trust in Guatemala. The catch is paperwork: the mandate has to be granted in a public deed, and if you sign it abroad it must be apostilled, translated into Spanish under oath, protocolized before a Guatemalan notary and registered in the Registro Electrónico de Poderes. Done properly, it takes two to four weeks and costs a few hundred dollars, not thousands.

## Can a foreigner buy Guatemalan property through a power of attorney?

Yes. Foreigners can own property in Guatemala with no residency requirement and no minimum investment, and you do not have to sign in person. The law does require the mandate to exist in a public deed, so a Guatemalan notary is involved either way.

For a purchase, use a mandato especial con representación with an express clause authorising your representative to acquire real estate in your name. For a sale, it is the mirror image: a special mandate with a clause to sell and to sign the escritura pública. A generic letter saying "I authorise my lawyer to handle everything" will not be accepted by the Registro General de la Propiedad.

## Which type of power do you actually need?

Pick the narrowest one that still does the job. Guatemalan law recognises three, and the scope is the whole risk.

| Type | What it authorises | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Poder especial | One defined act: this purchase, this property, this sale | A single transaction. Ends when the act is completed |
| Poder general | An indefinite series of acts over time | Rental management, taxes, admin. Needs an express clause to sell, mortgage or encumber |
| Poder judicial | Representation before the courts | Litigation. Must be substituted in a Guatemalan lawyer if your agent is not one |

A general power without the special clause cannot sell your property, and that clause is the one remote sellers most often forget.

## How do you grant a power of attorney from abroad?

You have two routes.

**Route 1: at a Guatemalan consulate.** Guatemalan consular officials who are also notaries can authorise acts abroad that take effect in Guatemala. The power takes effect only once it is protocolized here, so the consulate is the start of the chain, not the end of it.

**Route 2: before a foreign notary, then apostille and protocolization.** This is the route most remote buyers use:

1. Have the deed drafted in Spanish first. Your Guatemalan lawyer usually prepares it and sends you the Spanish text.
2. Sign it before a notary in your own country, who certifies your signature and identity.
3. Get it apostilled. Guatemala joined the Hague Apostille Convention in 2016, in force here from 18 September 2017, so an apostille replaces consular legalisation.
4. Have it sworn-translated into Spanish if it is not already in Spanish. A translator authorised in Guatemala must do it, and documents translated elsewhere are routinely rejected.
5. Protocolize it before a Guatemalan notary and register it. Article 38 of the Ley del Organismo Judicial requires powers from abroad to be protocolized here, and the notary notifies the Archivo General de Protocolos within ten working days.

If your country signed the older Inter-American Convention on powers of attorney (Mexico, Colombia, Peru, Chile, Brazil and most of Central and South America), that route applies instead of the apostille, with slightly different requirements. Tell your Guatemalan lawyer which country you will sign from before they draft anything.

## What does a power of attorney cost in Guatemala?

Notarial fees are freely negotiable under Article 106 of the Código de Notariado. Where no fee is agreed, the statutory default for a deed of indeterminate value is Q200 to Q5,000. The fixed government charges are small and predictable:

| Item | 2026 cost |
|---|---|
| Notary fee for granting the power | Q200 to Q5,000, negotiated |
| Timbre notarial (notarial stamp) | Q10 |
| Fiscal stamp, first page of the testimonio | Q10 general power, Q2 special power |
| Papel sellado especial para protocolos | Q10 per sheet |
| Apostille in your home country | Varies by state, roughly USD 5 to 100 |
| Sworn translation in Guatemala | Around Q300 |
| Registration in the Registro Electrónico de Poderes | Q130 |

For a single purchase, budget a few hundred US dollars all in. Anyone quoting four figures for a straightforward special mandate is padding the number.

## What can your representative do in your name?

A well-drafted special mandate lets your apoderado sign the promesa de compraventa and the escritura pública de compraventa, apply for your NIT tax number at the SAT, pay transfer taxes and municipal fees, register the deed at the Registro General de la Propiedad, and hand over the property on completion. What it should not do is give your lawyer the power to borrow against the property, sell it to a third party or encumber it. Read the clause list before you sign, not after.

## What are the risks of buying remotely?

**Power of attorney abuse.** When you are the buyer, the person who shows up to close may be acting for a seller who never granted them anything. Fraudulent powers are one of the most common scams in Guatemalan real estate, so your lawyer should verify the document at the Archivo General de Protocolos before money moves, never from a photocopy.

**Powers that are too broad.** A general power with a selling clause is a loaded weapon if the relationship sours. Use a narrow special mandate for one deal.

**Powers that do not expire conveniently.** A mandate ends when the act is done, when you revoke it, or when you die. It does not end when your agent changes firm, so review it every couple of years.

**Unregistered powers.** If a power granted abroad is never protocolized and registered here, the registry will not act on it. It has to be the whole chain, not just the sign-and-apostille part.

## How do you revoke a power of attorney?

Revoke it in a public deed before a notary, register the revocation in the Registro Electrónico de Poderes, and confirm it to your representative in writing. Until it is registered, a third party acting in good faith can still rely on the original power, which is why a narrow mandate matters more than a fast one.
