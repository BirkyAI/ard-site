# Can You Buy Antigua Guatemala Property with Crypto?

Yes, in practice, but not the way most buyers imagine it. You can own cryptocurrency in Guatemala, and you can use the value of that crypto to buy a house in Antigua, but no bank and no seller is obliged to accept Bitcoin, Ethereum or USDT directly. Almost every crypto-funded purchase here happens by converting your coins into US dollars or quetzales through a regulated exchange before closing, so the property transfer itself stays in the normal legal channel.

Here is how it actually works, what it costs, and where buyers get stuck.

## Is cryptocurrency legal in Guatemala?

Holding, buying and selling cryptocurrency is legal in Guatemala. The quetzal is the only official currency, and the Banco de Guatemala does not recognise crypto as a means of payment, so no merchant and no seller can be forced to accept it. Regulated exchanges operate under know-your-customer rules, which means you need a valid ID, either your passport or your DPI, plus a selfie, before you can fund an account and move real money.

For property, that leads to one practical conclusion: in Guatemala crypto behaves like an asset you convert, not like cash you hand across the table.

## Will an Antigua seller accept Bitcoin or stablecoins?

Occasionally, but it is the exception. Antigua's sellers are mostly Guatemalan families, long-time estate owners and small developers. They price in US dollars, they expect dollars or quetzales at closing, and their lawyers want a clean, documented paper trail for the Registro General de la Propiedad.

A handful of sellers, usually foreign owners who already hold crypto themselves, will agree to receive USDT or Bitcoin. Even then, the price is quoted in fiat and the crypto leg is settled at the exchange rate on the day of payment. Treat "crypto accepted" as a negotiation point on one specific property, never as a rule for the whole market.

## How a crypto-funded purchase in Antigua actually works

The clean route has six steps, and it is the route your lawyer will recommend.

1. Open an account with a KYC-regulated exchange that supports settlement in US dollars and quetzales.
2. Sell enough crypto to cover the full purchase price plus closing costs, usually 5 to 10 percent of the price.
3. Withdraw the proceeds to a Guatemalan or US bank account in fiat money.
4. Sign the promise-to-sell agreement, the promesa de compraventa, and pay your deposit in dollars or quetzales.
5. Have the notary draft the escritura pública stating the real purchase price.
6. Pay the balance, sign before the notary, and register the title at the Registro General de la Propiedad.

Step 5 is the one that matters most. Whatever currency you started with, the money recorded at the registry has to be money the seller can bank and the notary can document.

## Comparing the payment routes

| Route | How it works | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank wire in USD or GTQ | Convert crypto at an exchange, then wire fiat to the seller | Almost every purchase, the standard route |
| Direct crypto to the seller | Seller accepts USDT or BTC and settles at the day's rate | Rare, crypto-holding foreign sellers |
| Crypto-collateral loan | Pledge coins to a lender, borrow fiat against them | Buyers who refuse to sell their holdings |
| Third-party settlement service | A processor converts and pays the seller in fiat | Buyers who want a single invoice trail |

## What about crypto mortgages in Guatemala?

They exist on paper, mostly through foreign fintech lenders, and not through Guatemala's major banks. As of 2026 no mainstream Guatemalan bank lends against Bitcoin, and what is marketed as a crypto mortgage is usually a foreign lender taking your coins as collateral and paying you out in fiat. The rates and terms are worse than a local mortgage, and that lender sits outside Guatemalan banking supervision, so your recourse if something goes wrong is limited. For most buyers, selling a slice of your holdings and paying cash beats financing against a volatile asset.

## Taxes and paperwork you cannot skip

Two points, and both are non-negotiable.

First, crypto gains can be taxable. Guatemala's tax authority, the SAT, treats profit from the sale of assets as potentially taxable income, so if your coins gained value before you sold them, talk to a Guatemalan accountant about how that is declared.

Second, the escritura pública must state the real price you paid. Declaring a lower figure to shrink transfer taxes is fraud, it can invalidate your title, and every serious notary will refuse to do it. Plan on closing costs of roughly 5 to 10 percent of the purchase price, and pay those from the same clean fiat account as everything else.

## The risks I warn buyers about

- **Volatility between offer and closing.** A deal can take 60 to 90 days, and your crypto balance can move a lot in that window. Convert early, not on the morning of closing.
- **Source-of-funds questions.** Guatemalan banks and notaries increasingly ask where large sums came from. Keep your exchange statements, KYC records and withdrawal confirmations.
- **No reversal.** Crypto payments cannot be clawed back. Never send coins to a seller's wallet on a promise.
- **Fake exchanges.** Use established, KYC-regulated platforms with a verifiable legal entity, not whatever an agent recommends over WhatsApp.

## What I tell buyers who hold crypto

Crypto is a fine way to hold wealth, and a workable way to fund an Antigua purchase, as long as you treat it as a conversion step rather than a payment method. Convert to fiat through a regulated exchange, document every move, and let the property transaction run through a notary and the registry exactly as it would for a cash buyer.
