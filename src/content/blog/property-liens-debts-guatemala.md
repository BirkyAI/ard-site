Every registered debt and restriction on a Guatemalan property sits in one document: the certificación registral from the Registro de la Propiedad. Order it, through your own notary, before you commit money. It names the registered owner, the measurements, and any hipoteca, gravamen, embargo, usufructo or registered lease attached to the property. A clean certification, plus paid municipal tax, is what makes a purchase safe. Anything listed there must be cleared at closing, not promised away.

## What is a gravamen in Guatemala?

A gravamen is a charge registered against the property itself, not against its owner. A registered charge travels with the land, so buying from a seller who has debts can mean inheriting the problem. Notaries list these in the certification under one heading: a mortgage (hipoteca), a court attachment (embargo), a right of use (usufructo), a registered lease (arrendamiento registrado), a family-homestead restriction (patrimonio familiar) and an easement (servidumbre). Some limit what you can do with the property; a few block a sale outright.

## Which charges can appear on a property?

| Charge | What it means | Blocks a sale? | How it is cleared |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hipoteca (mortgage) | The seller borrowed against the property and the bank holds a registered claim | Yes | The bank issues a finiquito and the notary cancels the entry at closing |
| Embargo (court order) | A judge has frozen the property over an unpaid debt or lawsuit | Yes | The debt is settled or the order lifted, then cancelled in the registry |
| Usufructo (right of use) | Someone else may live in or use the property for life or a set term | Usually | The holder renounces the right in writing before a notary |
| Arrendamiento registrado | A long lease on the registry, which binds a new owner | Sometimes | Wait out the term, negotiate an exit, or price it into the offer |
| Patrimonio familiar | A family restriction that keeps the home out of forced sale | Yes | Removed by a judge or with the beneficiaries' written consent |
| Servidumbre (easement) | A neighbour's right of passage or services across the land | No, but it stays | Read it, price it, and repeat it in the new escritura |

## How do you check a property for liens in Guatemala?

The check runs on two tracks, the registry and the municipality.

1. **Order a fresh certificación registral.** Your notary requests it from the Registro de la Propiedad. It is the only record that shows registered charges, and it runs roughly Q800 to Q2,500.
2. **Read it yourself.** Confirm the owner's name matches the seller, the measurements match the plano, and the gravámenes section is clean.
3. **Request the IUSI and solvencia certificates** at the Municipalidad de Antigua, which show whether property tax and municipal charges are paid.
4. **Check the utility accounts and any condo dues.** Empagua water, Energuate electricity and condominium maintenance all carry their own balances.
5. **Walk it and ask who lives there.** No paper reflects a tenant, a relative or a usufructuary in possession, and possession is the one problem a certification never shows.

## What debts never appear on the registry?

IUSI property tax, municipal arbitrios, water and electricity balances and condo dues are not registered charges, so they never appear in the certification. They are still not harmless: unpaid property tax follows the property in practice, and utility arrears block the account transfer. Verify each one, and write into the promesa de venta that every outstanding bill is settled to the date of the sale.

## Can you buy a property that still has a mortgage?

Yes, and it is common. The seller's bank releases its hipoteca through a finiquito, and the notary normally deducts the pay-off from the price at closing. Two habits keep this safe: never hand over the full price before the mortgage is cancelled, and set the mechanism out in the promesa, including what happens if the release is delayed.

## What does "libre de gravámenes" actually mean?

The escritura and the promesa usually say the property is sold libre de gravámenes, free of liens and encumbrances. Treat that as a promise, not a fact: the fact is the certification, dated as close to closing as possible. Guatemala has no title insurance, so the notary's registry search and the registration in your name are the protection you hold. Read the testimonio before you release the final payment.

## What happens if a lien appears after you buy?

A registered charge attaches to the property, so in the worst case you pay to clear it and pursue the seller afterwards. That is why a stale or forged certification is a serious problem, and why you order your own rather than trusting one handed across the table. Watch for a forged escritura, a fraudulent power of attorney, or the same property sold twice. ## A short checklist before you commit

1. A fresh certificación registral, ordered by your own notary.
2. The gravámenes section read line by line.
3. IUSI and solvencia certificates from the municipality.
4. Water, power and any condo dues verified as current.
5. A written answer on who lives in the property.
6. Every charge named in the promesa, with the deposit conditional on a clean title.
7. The testimonio read, and registration confirmed, before the final payment.

### Do you need title insurance in Guatemala?

There is no title-insurance market in Guatemala, so there is nothing to buy. Protection comes from the notary's registry search, a current certification, and registration in your own name. The [closing process](/blog/guatemala-real-estate-closing-process/) shows where each step fits.

### Can a foreigner buy a property that has a lien on it?

Yes. Foreigners hold title on the same terms as citizens, and a lien can be cleared at closing. What you should never accept is a verbal promise that it will be handled later.
